# CABG / Heart Bypass Surgery in India: Cost, Hospitals, Surgeons, Recovery & International Patient Guide

**18–25 minute resource guide**

Being told that you may need heart bypass surgery can make every decision feel urgent at once. Before you compare hospitals or flights, ask your current cardiology team how urgent the situation is, what your angiogram shows and whether CABG, angioplasty or medical treatment are the options actually being compared in your case.

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), often called heart bypass surgery, creates new routes for blood to flow around narrowed or blocked coronary arteries. It is one of several ways coronary artery disease may be managed. Depending on coronary anatomy, symptoms and medical context, other options may include medicines and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI/angioplasty and stenting).

The American Heart Association’s coronary revascularization guidance emphasizes patient-centred shared decision-making and recommends a multidisciplinary **Heart Team** when the best revascularization strategy is unclear. That principle is especially important for an international patient: the question is not simply “Where can I get cheaper bypass surgery?” It is “What is the right revascularization strategy for this coronary anatomy and patient, and which team can deliver and follow it safely?”

# Important: do not delay emergency cardiac care for travel

New or severe chest pressure/pain, severe breathlessness, collapse/fainting or rapidly worsening symptoms can require urgent local assessment. A person with a current heart attack or unstable condition may need immediate local treatment rather than an international comparison process.

Before planning travel, ask your current treating cardiologist whether you are medically stable enough to fly and whether waiting for an overseas opinion is clinically acceptable.

# Quick answer

CABG uses blood vessels from elsewhere in the body to bypass obstructed coronary arteries so blood can reach heart muscle beyond a blockage. AHA patient resources distinguish CABG as an operating-room procedure from PCI, which is usually catheter-based.

The choice between CABG, PCI and medical management depends on the coronary anatomy, disease complexity, symptoms, heart function, diabetes and other medical conditions. Current revascularization guidance recommends a Heart Team approach when the optimal strategy is uncertain.

A current CABG estimate should be tied to your angiogram, heart function, proposed operation and expected ICU and ward stay. Treat undated public ranges as orientation only; they are not a budget for your case.

International patients should compare the exact proposed operation, surgeon, graft strategy, ICU/ward plan, major exclusions, rehabilitation, local recovery, flight clearance and long-term follow-up.

# 1. What is coronary artery disease?

Coronary arteries supply the heart muscle. Atherosclerotic disease can narrow these arteries and reduce blood flow.

Possible symptoms include:
- chest pressure or pain;
- exertional breathlessness;
- reduced exercise capacity;
- fatigue;
- atypical symptoms.

Some people are diagnosed after an acute coronary syndrome; others after investigation of stable symptoms. Symptoms alone do not determine whether CABG is appropriate. Coronary anatomy and the rest of the clinical picture matter.

---

# 2. CABG, PCI/stents or medicines?

This is the real treatment decision behind many searches for “CABG in India.”

## Medical management
Can include anti-anginal treatment, antiplatelet therapy, lipid lowering, blood-pressure management, diabetes care, smoking cessation and other interventions selected by the clinical team.

## PCI / angioplasty
A catheter-based procedure used to open selected narrowed or blocked coronary arteries, commonly with stent placement.

## CABG
A surgical procedure using graft vessels to create alternate routes around obstructed coronary arteries.

## What guidelines emphasize
The ACC/AHA/SCAI revascularization guideline emphasizes:
- clinical-indication-based decisions;
- shared decision-making;
- Heart Team input when the optimum revascularization strategy is unclear.

It also describes clinical/anatomic settings in which surgical or percutaneous revascularization may be favoured.

Do not turn this into a universal “CABG beats stents” or “stents beat CABG” marketing claim.

# 5. What happens during CABG?

In simplified terms:

1. the patient receives anaesthesia;
2. the cardiac surgery team accesses the heart;
3. suitable blood vessels are prepared as grafts;
4. grafts are connected to create routes around blocked arteries;
5. the operation is completed and the patient moves to intensive post-operative monitoring.

Different surgical techniques exist. Some operations use cardiopulmonary bypass; selected cases may use off-pump techniques. Some centres offer minimally invasive approaches for selected patients/anatomy.

Availability of a technique does not mean it is suitable for every patient.

# 8. Hospital stay and recovery

CABG is major surgery. The exact inpatient course depends on:
- age;
- heart function;
- urgency;
- number/complexity of grafts;
- associated valve/aortic procedures;
- kidney/lung disease;
- diabetes;
- bleeding;
- infection;
- rhythm problems;
- rehabilitation progress.

Recovery milestones are more useful than a rigid day count:

- stable circulation and breathing;
- ICU discharge;
- walking/mobility;
- nutrition;
- wound care;
- medication transition;
- safe hospital discharge;
- early follow-up;
- rehabilitation;
- travel clearance;
- long-term cardiology follow-up.

# 9. Long-term care after CABG

CABG improves blood flow but does not erase the underlying atherosclerotic disease.

Current AHA guidance emphasizes long-term secondary prevention and cardiac rehabilitation for eligible patients. Medication selection and duration are individualized.

For content and conversion, the important point is:

**Do not market CABG as a one-time trip with no follow-up.**

The return-home plan should include:
- medication list;
- cardiac rehabilitation;
- risk-factor management;
- wound review;
- local cardiologist;
- warning signs;
- Indian hospital contact.

# 17. FAQ

## Is CABG the same as open-heart surgery?
CABG is one type of heart surgery. Open-heart surgery is a broader term that can also refer to valve and other operations.

## CABG or angioplasty: which is better?
There is no universal answer. Anatomy and patient factors matter. Revascularization guidelines emphasize patient-centred decisions and Heart Team input when the optimal strategy is unclear.

## How much does CABG cost in India?
There is no single reliable amount for every case. Request a current written estimate that identifies the operation, records reviewed, expected stay, inclusions, exclusions and factors that would trigger re-estimation.

## What record is most important?
Coronary angiogram images/report are usually central, alongside echocardiography and clinical records.

## Can I travel immediately after being told I need CABG?
Only if the treating clinicians believe the patient is stable enough and the delay is acceptable. Urgent disease may require local treatment.

## How soon can I fly home after CABG?
There is no universal Canopus rule. The treating surgeon/cardiologist should clear travel based on recovery and individual risk.

# 22. What if two hospitals disagree: CABG versus PCI?

Do not treat disagreement as a sales problem.

Create a structured clinical-question sheet:

1. Are both teams looking at the same angiogram?
2. Are they using the same assessment of disease complexity?
3. Is left-main disease present?
4. What is the heart function?
5. Does diabetes materially affect the decision?
6. Is complete revascularization achievable with either strategy?
7. What does each team see as the major short- and long-term trade-offs?
8. Has a multidisciplinary Heart Team discussed the case?
9. Is the decision urgent?
10. What patient goals/preferences matter?

The patient can take this sheet into a cardiology/cardiac-surgery consultation.

Canopus organizes the disagreement; clinicians resolve it.

# 28. Pre-travel cardiac readiness discussion

A hospital invitation letter and a flight booking do not establish that a cardiac patient is medically ready to travel. Before departure, the patient should have a clinician-led discussion that covers the actual journey.

Useful questions include:

- Is the patient currently stable?
- Has there been recent chest pain at rest or rapidly worsening symptoms?
- Was there a recent acute coronary syndrome?
- Is the proposed delay before treatment acceptable?
- Does the patient need wheelchair or airport assistance?
- Are there restrictions on carrying medicines or medical equipment?
- Does the patient need a medical letter for the airline?
- What should happen if symptoms worsen in transit?
- Which medicines should be taken on the travel day?
- Who is the emergency contact at the receiving hospital?

Canopus should treat this as a **readiness checkpoint**, not as an automatic approval. The treating clinicians own the medical decision.

# 29. What families should know about estimate changes after arrival

A remote estimate is based on the information available before travel. It can change when the receiving team finds:

- a different coronary anatomy than expected;
- a need for additional testing;
- kidney, lung or other medical issues that change management;
- an additional valve/aortic problem;
- longer critical-care needs;
- infection or another acute issue;
- a change in the recommended revascularization strategy.

The lead page should explain this before the user submits a case. Transparency can improve conversion quality because families who continue are better prepared for the real process.

A good estimate interface should therefore show:

**Indicative plan** → **in-person assessment** → **confirmed plan** → **final bill**

rather than presenting one pre-travel number as a guaranteed package.
