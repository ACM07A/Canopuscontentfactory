# Heart Valve Surgery in India: Repair, Replacement, TAVR, Costs, Hospitals & International Patient Guide

**18–26 minute resource guide**

If a cardiologist has told you that a heart valve is narrowed or leaking, the difficult part is often understanding how serious it is and why one team suggests monitoring, another repair and another replacement. Start with the exact valve, the severity shown on your echocardiogram, the symptoms it may explain and how urgently your current team believes it needs attention.

Heart valve disease is not one operation. Narrowing is called **stenosis**; leakage is called **regurgitation**. Disease can affect the aortic, mitral, tricuspid or pulmonary valve, and treatment choices differ according to severity, symptoms, heart function, age, anatomy and other medical conditions.

The 2020 ACC/AHA guideline on valvular heart disease says patients with **severe valve disease being considered for intervention should be evaluated by a multidisciplinary team**, with referral to or consultation with a Primary or Comprehensive Valve Center. For severe aortic stenosis, the choice between surgical and transcatheter valve replacement should be a shared decision considering lifetime risks and benefits.

That is the right framework for an international patient. Before comparing prices, compare the diagnosis, severity, whether repair or replacement is proposed, whether a surgical or catheter-based approach is appropriate, valve type/device, surgeon/interventional team, anticoagulation implications, recovery, and long-term follow-up.

# Urgent symptoms come before travel planning

Severe breathlessness, chest pain, fainting, rapidly worsening fluid retention or other acute cardiac symptoms can require urgent local assessment.

Do not delay medically necessary local cardiac care simply to obtain international quotes. Ask your current cardiologist whether you are stable enough for travel and how time-sensitive the valve intervention is.

# Quick answer

Heart valve treatment can include monitoring/medical management, surgical valve repair, surgical valve replacement and, in selected disease/anatomy, transcatheter procedures such as TAVR/TAVI. The correct option depends on the valve, lesion, severity, symptoms, ventricular response and patient factors.

“Heart valve surgery cost” cannot be one number. Repair, replacement and transcatheter procedures use different teams, devices and stay assumptions. Ask for a current written estimate tied to your records, the exact procedure and the proposed valve or device.

# 1. What heart valves do

The heart has four valves that direct blood flow:
- aortic;
- mitral;
- tricuspid;
- pulmonary.

Valve disease can make a valve:
- too narrow to open effectively (**stenosis**);
- unable to close effectively (**regurgitation/leakage**);
- affected by a combination of problems.

The consequences depend on which valve is involved and how severe the abnormality is.

---

# 2. Symptoms that may occur

Valve disease can be associated with:
- shortness of breath;
- reduced exercise capacity;
- fatigue;
- chest discomfort;
- dizziness/fainting;
- palpitations;
- swelling/fluid retention.

Some people have severe disease with limited symptoms. Others have symptoms from other causes. Symptoms alone cannot identify the valve lesion or determine the correct intervention.

---

# 3. Diagnosis and severity

The ACC/AHA guideline emphasizes correlating:
- history;
- physical examination;
- ECG/chest assessment;
- transthoracic echocardiogram.

If findings and initial testing do not fit together, further assessment may include:
- transesophageal echocardiography;
- CT;
- cardiac MRI;
- stress testing;
- cardiac catheterization.

For an international patient, the hospital should not make a definitive intervention decision from “valve leaking badly” in a message. Send the actual echo and supporting records.

# 5. The first decision: does the valve need intervention now?

The guideline stages valve disease based on symptoms, anatomy, severity, ventricular and pulmonary response.

The treatment decision can depend on:
- valve;
- stenosis vs regurgitation;
- severity;
- symptoms;
- ventricular function;
- progression;
- pulmonary pressures;
- other cardiac surgery needs;
- age/life expectancy;
- comorbidities;
- anatomy;
- patient preferences.

Canopus should not turn an echo value into an automated “eligible for surgery” outcome.

# 6. Repair versus replacement

## Valve repair
The surgeon preserves the patient’s valve and reconstructs it.

Repair is especially relevant in selected mitral/tricuspid disease, depending on anatomy and surgical expertise.

Questions:
- Is repair feasible?
- How durable does the team expect it to be for this lesion?
- What would make the surgeon convert to replacement?
- How often does this team perform this repair type?

## Valve replacement
The diseased valve is replaced with a prosthetic valve.

A replacement can involve:
- surgical implantation;
- transcatheter implantation for selected conditions/anatomy.

The choice is not simply “new valve versus old valve”; long-term implications matter.

---

# 7. Mechanical versus bioprosthetic surgical valves

Valve type is a shared clinical decision.

The ACC/AHA guideline highlights lifetime risks and benefits when choosing valve strategy.

A patient should discuss:
- durability;
- age and expected lifespan;
- anticoagulation;
- bleeding/clot risk;
- pregnancy considerations where relevant;
- possibility of future valve procedures;
- monitoring;
- access to medication and INR testing at home when relevant.

Do not publish a universal age cutoff as a Canopus rule.

For international patients, medication monitoring after returning home is a practical part of valve choice.

---

# 8. TAVR/TAVI versus surgical aortic valve replacement

TAVR/TAVI inserts a prosthetic aortic valve by catheter rather than standard open surgical replacement.

The ACC/AHA guideline says the choice of transcatheter versus surgical prosthesis for severe aortic stenosis should be shared decision-making based on lifetime risks/benefits and patient factors.

Relevant considerations may include:
- age/life expectancy;
- anatomy;
- surgical risk;
- vascular access;
- need for other cardiac surgery;
- coronary anatomy;
- valve durability strategy;
- pacemaker/other procedural risks;
- future interventions.

Do not market TAVR as simply “newer and therefore better.”

---

# 9. Mitral valve disease: repair, replacement and catheter options

Mitral regurgitation can arise from different mechanisms. The treatment plan may include:
- surveillance/medical management;
- surgical repair;
- surgical replacement;
- selected transcatheter edge-to-edge repair or other approaches depending on anatomy and indication.

A hospital response should specify:
- the mechanism of regurgitation;
- severity;
- ventricular function;
- whether repair is expected;
- what device/approach is planned;
- backup plan if repair is not feasible.

Apollo currently publishes dedicated MitraClip and surgical/robotic mitral procedure pages. Availability does not establish suitability for a particular patient.

# 12. Recovery and follow-up

Open cardiac surgery and transcatheter intervention have very different recovery profiles.

Do not use one “valve surgery recovery time.”

For any pathway, important milestones can include:
- stable circulation/breathing;
- rhythm monitoring;
- wound/access-site care;
- mobility;
- medication plan;
- anticoagulation/antiplatelet plan where appropriate;
- repeat echo;
- rehabilitation;
- follow-up;
- travel clearance.

For a mechanical prosthetic valve, long-term anticoagulation/monitoring can become a central return-home issue. Exact medication instructions must come from the treating team.

---

# 13. Risks to discuss

Depending on procedure and patient:
- bleeding;
- infection;
- stroke;
- rhythm disturbance;
- kidney injury;
- vascular complications;
- pacemaker need;
- prosthetic valve complications;
- clotting/bleeding from anticoagulation;
- reoperation/reintervention;
- anaesthetic/medical complications.

The hospital should explain risks specific to the proposed intervention.

# 14. Heart valve treatment cost in India

Do not accept a single “valve package” without the clinical and device assumptions behind it.

## Ask for a current procedure-specific estimate

The estimate should identify the valve and lesion, repair or replacement plan, surgical or transcatheter approach, assumed prosthetic valve or device, expected ICU and ward stay, professional fees, medicines and consumables, major exclusions and validity date. Public figures from different pages may describe different devices and package scopes, so use the hospital's current written response to your records as the comparison document.

## Why price varies
- valve/lesion;
- repair vs replacement;
- one vs multiple valves;
- surgical vs transcatheter;
- prosthetic valve/device;
- combined CABG/aortic procedure;
- ICU/ward days;
- surgeon/team;
- hospital/city;
- tests;
- complications;
- room;
- medicines/consumables.

# 25. Why valve pages need a device-aware cost model

A valve estimate can be distorted if the content system treats every prosthesis as one generic line item.

Canopus should capture:
- valve affected;
- repair vs replacement;
- surgical vs transcatheter;
- mechanical vs bioprosthetic surgical valve where relevant;
- transcatheter device where relevant;
- single vs double valve;
- additional CABG/aortic surgery;
- consumables/device included or excluded.

For transcatheter procedures, device cost can represent a major portion of the commercial estimate. Therefore a “hospital package” number without the device assumption is incomplete.

---

# 26. How to compare a repair proposal with a replacement proposal

If one centre proposes repair and another replacement, do not use a price-sorting table.

Ask each team:

1. What exact valve pathology is present?
2. What makes repair feasible or not feasible?
3. What is the expected durability strategy?
4. What intra-operative findings could change the plan?
5. If repair fails or is not feasible, what is the backup replacement plan?
6. Which prosthesis would be used?
7. What anticoagulation implications follow?
8. Is there a meaningful difference in expected recovery?
9. Does the centre have a dedicated valve-repair program?
10. Can the case be reviewed jointly by imaging, interventional cardiology and cardiac surgery?

Canopus should display the disagreement prominently rather than collapsing both into “heart valve surgery.”
