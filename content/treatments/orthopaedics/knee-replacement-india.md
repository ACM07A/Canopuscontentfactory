# Knee Replacement in India: Cost, Hospitals, Doctors, Recovery & International Patient Guide

**18–24 minute resource guide**

Knee replacement is usually considered when knee damage—often from arthritis—causes substantial pain, stiffness or loss of function and non-surgical treatment is no longer giving enough relief. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) describes common reasons for considering total knee replacement as severe activity-limiting pain or stiffness, pain at rest, chronic swelling, deformity, and inadequate improvement with non-operative treatments. The decision is individual and should be made with an orthopaedic surgeon.

For an international patient, deciding on surgery is only one part of the problem. You need to understand the diagnosis, which operation is actually being proposed, the implant, surgeon, rehabilitation plan, hospital package, likely local stay, when your treating team considers you fit to travel, and what follow-up happens after you return home.

# Quick answer

A total knee replacement removes damaged joint surfaces and replaces them with prosthetic components. AAOS describes total knee replacement as an established procedure that can relieve pain, correct deformity and improve function for appropriately selected patients.

The most important international-patient question is not simply “Which Indian hospital is cheapest?” It is whether the proposals you are comparing are based on the same diagnosis and same operation. Compare the surgeon, one knee vs both, total vs partial replacement, implant system, robotic/navigation use if proposed, anaesthesia, expected stay, rehabilitation, exclusions and follow-up.

Public prices are only a reference. Apollo Hospitals currently publishes roughly **₹1.5–3.0 lakh** for total knee replacement on its general procedure page, while a separate page for knee replacement in an obese patient cites **₹2.5–5.0 lakh**. The difference itself is useful: complexity, implant, hospital, patient factors and package scope can materially change price. A written current hospital estimate based on your records is more useful than a generic national “average.”

# If you have urgent symptoms

Knee osteoarthritis is usually not an emergency, but urgent local medical assessment is more important than international travel planning if there is:

- a hot, very swollen knee with fever;
- a major recent injury with deformity or inability to bear weight;
- sudden calf swelling;
- unexplained new shortness of breath or chest pain;
- rapidly worsening redness, drainage or infection after recent surgery;
- severe new neurological symptoms after trauma.

If you recently had knee replacement and develop sudden breathlessness or chest pain, seek urgent local care.

# 1. Why knee replacement may be considered

Knee replacement should not be chosen because an X-ray “looks bad” in isolation. A surgeon looks at the relationship between:

- pain;
- stiffness;
- walking;
- stairs;
- sleep/rest pain;
- work and daily function;
- swelling;
- deformity;
- imaging;
- response to non-surgical treatment;
- overall health;
- expectations.

AAOS notes that people who often benefit from replacement have severe pain or stiffness limiting daily activity, pain at rest, persistent inflammation, deformity, or insufficient improvement from other treatments.

This matters when comparing Indian hospital proposals. If one surgeon recommends total knee replacement while another proposes continued conservative treatment or partial replacement, understand **why the interpretations differ** before comparing price.

# 2. Symptoms that commonly lead to an orthopaedic evaluation

Symptoms can include:

- knee pain while walking;
- difficulty using stairs;
- stiffness after sitting or sleeping;
- reduced walking distance;
- pain while sitting or lying down;
- swelling;
- clicking or grinding;
- bowing inward or outward;
- reduced range of motion;
- difficulty with work, exercise or self-care.

These symptoms are not specific to one diagnosis. Osteoarthritis is common, but inflammatory arthritis, prior injury, meniscal problems, osteonecrosis, infection and other conditions can also produce pain.

A resource page should help the reader prepare for evaluation, not diagnose the cause.

# 4. Non-surgical treatment and surgery are not the same decision

Depending on the diagnosis, non-operative care may include:

- activity modification;
- weight management where relevant;
- physiotherapy and strengthening;
- walking aids;
- pain-relief medicine;
- anti-inflammatory treatment when appropriate;
- selected injections;
- treatment of related hip/spine/foot problems.

Surgery becomes a discussion when symptoms remain significant and an orthopaedic surgeon believes the anatomy/pathology is appropriate.

| Path | Typical role | Potential advantage | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physiotherapy/exercise | strength, mobility, function | non-operative | may not overcome advanced structural disease |
| Medication | symptom control | accessible | benefit and side effects vary |
| Injection | selected symptom relief | minimally invasive | effect can be temporary/variable |
| Partial replacement | selected compartment disease | preserves more native joint | not appropriate for every knee |
| Total replacement | advanced symptomatic joint disease | established surgical option | major operation and rehabilitation |
| Revision replacement | failed/problematic previous replacement | treats implant failure | more complex than primary replacement |

This table is educational, not a treatment recommendation.

# 5. What happens during total knee replacement?

AAOS describes the operation broadly as:

1. preparing damaged bone and cartilage;
2. positioning metal components on the femur and tibia;
3. resurfacing the patella in selected cases;
4. placing a plastic spacer between metal components for smooth movement.

Actual technique and implant choice vary by patient and surgeon.

Questions worth asking:

- What exact implant system is proposed?
- Why is it suitable for this case?
- Cemented or another fixation approach?
- Is patellar resurfacing planned?
- Conventional, computer-navigated or robotic assistance?
- What does that technology change?
- Is the technology cost included?
- What backup implant options are available if surgery differs from the plan?

---

# 6. Robotic knee replacement: useful tool, not autonomous surgery

“Robotic knee replacement” can sound as if a robot performs the operation independently. In practice, robotic or computer-assisted systems are surgeon-controlled tools for planning, alignment or execution.

Ask:

- which system is used;
- what part of the operation it assists;
- how often the surgeon uses it;
- whether cost is included;
- whether it changes implant choice;
- whether the same operation would be recommended without it;
- what evidence is relevant to this particular case.

Do not choose a hospital only because the marketing page says “robotic.”

Hospitals may repeat/add:

- anaesthesia assessment;
- infection screening;
- other clearance.

A plan can change if new findings appear.

## Phase 6 — Surgery and inpatient recovery

Typical milestones:
- pain control;
- getting out of bed;
- assisted walking;
- physiotherapy;
- wound monitoring;
- blood-clot prevention/monitoring;
- stairs where needed;
- safe discharge.

AAOS describes discharge as based on medical and functional milestones.

## Phase 7 — Local recovery

**Hospital discharge ≠ automatically fit to fly.**

Ask:
- When is the wound review?
- When are staples/sutures handled if used?
- How much physiotherapy?
- What warning signs require hospital review?
- When will the surgeon assess travel readiness?
- Is airport assistance needed?
- What clot-prevention plan applies?

## Phase 8 — Return home

Carry:
- discharge summary;
- operation note;
- implant labels/details;
- imaging;
- medication list;
- rehab plan;
- wound instructions;
- clot-prevention instructions;
- follow-up date;
- emergency contact;
- invoices/payment records if needed.

# 8. Recovery: use milestones, not promises

AAOS emphasizes gradual return to activity. Discharge goals may include safe pain control, walking with an assistive device, eating/drinking, bathroom use, basic stairs when needed and ability to perform prescribed exercises.

A better timeline is:

```text
Surgery
  ↓
safe mobilization
  ↓
hospital discharge
  ↓
local wound + rehab review
  ↓
surgeon-approved travel
  ↓
home physiotherapy
  ↓
progressive return to activity
  ↓
long-term joint follow-up
```

Do not publish a universal “fly home on day 7” claim.

# 9. Risks and complications to discuss

Important topics include:

- infection;
- blood clot;
- bleeding/transfusion;
- anaesthesia complications;
- stiffness;
- persistent pain;
- nerve/vessel injury;
- implant loosening/wear;
- fracture;
- instability;
- need for revision.

AAOS patient guidance advises knowing warning signs of infection and blood clot after discharge.

For an international patient, an extra question matters:

> **What is the plan if a complication begins after I return home?**

The hospital should explain how the surgeon communicates with a home physician and when urgent local care takes priority.

# 11. How to shortlist a knee-replacement hospital

Use evidence, not a generic “top 10.” Ask each hospital to identify the exact branch, the joint-replacement service and the consultant assigned after reviewing your records. Verify the clinician's current appointment on the hospital's official directory; a name found in search results does not show that the person has accepted your case.

## Questions for every centre

- Who actually operates?
- Total or partial?
- One knee or both?
- Which implant?
- What if a different implant is required?
- Robotic/navigation included?
- How many physio sessions?
- What is excluded?
- What if stay is longer?
- Who reviews after return home?

# 14. Medical visa planning

India’s official e-Visa portal includes e-Medical and e-Medical Attendant categories and lists documents including the passport bio page and an Indian hospital letter for medical applications.

Before booking:

- passport validity;
- visa category;
- current duration and entries;
- hospital-letter requirements;
- attendant rules;

# 17. FAQ

## How much does knee replacement cost in India?
No single price applies. Apollo currently publishes a general TKR range of ₹1.5–3 lakh and a different ₹2.5–5 lakh range on a more complex/obesity-specific page. A current case-specific estimate is more useful.

## How long is hospital stay?
AAOS describes a typical 1–4 day stay, with some shorter pathways. International patients should separately plan local recovery.

## Can I fly immediately after discharge?
Do not assume so. The surgeon/medical team should assess travel readiness and blood-clot risk.

## Is robotic knee replacement better?
Robotic systems assist the surgeon. Whether they add meaningful benefit depends on system, surgeon, case and outcome being considered.

## Should I choose the cheapest hospital?
Compare diagnosis, procedure, implant, surgeon, package, exclusions, rehab, complications policy and follow-up—not only price.

## What reports should I send?
Start with recent orthopaedic notes, standing X-rays, relevant MRI, previous surgery records, medicines and major medical history.

No. Canopus coordinates records and hospital interactions. Clinicians make medical decisions.

# 23. Common comparison mistakes

## Mistake 1: comparing only the headline package
Fix: compare inclusions and assumptions.

## Mistake 2: assuming “robotic” means a different surgery
Fix: ask exactly how the technology is used and why.

## Mistake 3: assuming hospital discharge means airline readiness
Fix: ask the treating team separately about travel.

## Mistake 4: carrying only report PDFs but not actual images
Fix: where possible carry the image files requested by the surgeon.

## Mistake 5: ignoring previous surgery details
Fix: revision cases need prior operative and implant information when available.

## Mistake 6: booking the cheapest non-refundable flight
Fix: allow for clinical schedule changes.

## Mistake 7: having no home-country follow-up
Fix: identify a local orthopaedic/medical contact before returning.

# 24. How to judge whether an estimate is useful

A useful estimate is dated and tied to your records. It identifies unilateral or bilateral surgery, the implant assumption, hospital branch, room and stay assumptions, professional fees, routine tests and medicines, physiotherapy, follow-up and exclusions. It also states what may change after examination or updated testing.

Do not compare an estimate that includes the implant and rehabilitation with a headline figure that excludes them. Ask every hospital to clarify the same fields, then place the answers side by side. If a figure comes from aggregated public data rather than a current hospital response, check the date, sample size and scope before using it for your budget.
