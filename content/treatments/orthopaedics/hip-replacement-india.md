# Hip Replacement in India: Cost, Hospitals, Surgeons, Recovery & International Patient Guide

**18–25 minute resource guide**

If hip pain has turned walking, sleeping, dressing or using stairs into a daily calculation, you may be trying to decide whether replacement is the right next step and whether travelling for it is realistic. A useful opinion should connect what you feel and what you can no longer do with the examination and imaging, then explain what non-surgical options remain and what replacement would involve.

Total hip replacement replaces damaged parts of the hip joint with prosthetic components. It may be considered when hip disease causes substantial pain, stiffness and loss of function and non-surgical care is no longer providing enough relief. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) describes joint replacement as a treatment for damaged or arthritic joints when measures such as medication, physical therapy and activity modification do not adequately relieve pain and disability.

For an international patient, a good decision is not simply “Which hospital can do a hip replacement?” The important questions are: what is causing the hip problem, is replacement the correct treatment, what operation and implant are proposed, who will perform it, what additional health issues matter, how rehabilitation will work, what the written estimate includes, when the treating team considers air travel appropriate, and how follow-up will continue at home.

# Quick answer

A total hip replacement usually replaces the damaged femoral head with a prosthetic ball and stem and replaces the damaged socket with an artificial cup/liner. AAOS explains that total joint replacement removes damaged bone and cartilage and replaces them with metal, plastic and/or ceramic components.

AAOS’s current recovery guidance says a typical hospital stay after total hip replacement is about **1–2 days**, with some outpatient pathways allowing same-day discharge; actual discharge depends on pain control, mobility and other safety goals. An international patient needs additional time and a specific clinician decision about travel—hospital discharge should not be treated as automatic fit-to-fly clearance.

Apollo Hospitals currently publishes a broad total hip replacement cost range of approximately **₹1.5–4.0 lakh** on its general THR page and approximately **₹4–7 lakh** for robotic hip replacement on a separate procedure page. These are Apollo-published reference signals, not Canopus quotes and not a universal Indian market price.

# Urgent situations come before medical travel

Hip arthritis is often elective, but some hip problems require urgent local evaluation.

Seek appropriate local medical care rather than waiting for an overseas comparison if there is:
- a major fall or injury with inability to stand/walk;
- suspected fracture;
- a hot, very painful joint with fever;
- rapidly worsening wound redness/drainage after surgery;
- sudden severe calf swelling;
- sudden shortness of breath or chest pain;
- new neurological symptoms after trauma.

A fracture or acute infection has a different pathway from elective replacement for arthritis.

# 1. Symptoms that commonly lead to hip evaluation

Hip disease may cause:
- groin pain;
- outer hip or buttock pain;
- pain while walking;
- stiffness;
- difficulty putting on shoes/socks;
- reduced walking distance;
- difficulty getting up from a chair;
- limping;
- night/rest pain;
- reduced ability to work/exercise.

Not all “hip pain” comes from the hip joint. Spine disease, tendon problems, bursitis, fractures and other conditions can produce overlapping symptoms.

A clinician needs to determine the source.

# 2. Why total hip replacement may be considered

AAOS describes total joint replacement as a possible option when joint damage causes pain/disability and non-surgical treatments are no longer enough.

For a real patient, an orthopaedic surgeon may consider:
- symptoms;
- functional limitation;
- examination;
- X-rays/other imaging;
- diagnosis;
- duration of symptoms;
- response to physiotherapy/medicine/injections;
- age/activity;
- bone quality;
- prior hip operations;
- overall health;
- patient goals.

An X-ray should not be marketed as a “surgery score.”

# 3. Common diagnoses behind hip replacement discussions

## Osteoarthritis
Degeneration of articular cartilage can produce pain and stiffness.

## Inflammatory arthritis
Inflammatory disease can damage the joint.

## Osteonecrosis / avascular necrosis
Loss of blood supply can damage the femoral head.

## Previous trauma
Fracture or post-traumatic arthritis may alter anatomy and make reconstruction more complex.

## Hip fracture
Selected fractures, especially in older patients, may be treated with partial or total replacement, but this is a different clinical pathway from elective arthritis surgery and can be urgent.

## Developmental/anatomical problems
Previous dysplasia or childhood hip disease can make replacement technically more complex.

The exact diagnosis should appear in every hospital proposal.

# 5. Non-surgical care versus replacement

Treatment depends on diagnosis.

Potential non-operative measures can include:
- activity modification;
- physiotherapy;
- strengthening;
- walking aids;
- pain-relief medicine;
- anti-inflammatory treatment where clinically suitable;
- selected injections;
- weight management where relevant.

| Path | What it tries to do | Advantage | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Activity/physio | improve function and manage symptoms | avoids surgery | may not control advanced structural disease |
| Medicines | reduce symptoms | accessible | benefit/side effects vary |
| Injection | selected temporary symptom control | minimally invasive | not a durable solution for every condition |
| Hip resurfacing | selected alternative in carefully chosen patients | preserves more femoral bone | suitability is limited |
| Total hip replacement | replace damaged joint surfaces | established reconstructive procedure | major surgery + rehabilitation |
| Revision hip replacement | treat failed/problematic prior arthroplasty | addresses implant-related problem | often more complex/costly |

Do not publish a universal recommendation between these choices.

# 6. What happens during total hip replacement?

AAOS describes the hip as a ball-and-socket joint. In replacement:
- the damaged femoral head is removed;
- a prosthetic stem is inserted into the femur;
- a prosthetic ball replaces the femoral head;
- the damaged socket is prepared;
- a cup/liner is implanted in the pelvis.

Materials and fixation approaches vary.

Questions:
- What implant system?
- Ceramic/metal/polyethylene bearing combination?
- Cemented or uncemented components?
- Why is this chosen?
- What femoral/acetabular anatomy issues exist?
- Does previous surgery affect the plan?
- Is extra reconstruction needed?

# 7. Surgical approach: do not turn it into a marketing contest

Hospitals may advertise:
- posterior;
- lateral;
- anterior;
- minimally invasive;
- robotic;
- navigation-assisted approaches.

An approach is not automatically “better” for every patient.

Ask:
- what approach the surgeon normally uses;
- why it fits this anatomy;
- what precautions/rehab it changes;
- whether it affects implant selection;
- whether prior surgery changes the preferred approach;
- what the surgeon’s own protocol is.

Choose a surgeon/team, not a buzzword.

---

# 8. Robotic hip replacement

Robotic systems assist the surgeon with planning and/or execution. They do not independently diagnose the hip or perform surgery without the surgeon.

For international comparison:
- system name;
- what it measures;
- what steps it assists;
- surgeon’s experience with the system;
- additional cost;
- whether CT or additional imaging is required;
- whether the implant choice is constrained;
- why the surgeon believes it is useful for this case.

Apollo’s current robotic hip page publishes approximately ₹4–7 lakh. Treat that only as a current Apollo reference, not a universal price or evidence that robotic surgery is superior.

# 10. Hospital discharge and early recovery

AAOS currently states a typical hospital stay of 1–2 days, with outpatient same-day discharge in some pathways.

Before discharge, AAOS describes functional goals including:
- bed transfers;
- pain control;
- eating/drinking/bathroom;
- walking with an assistive device;
- stairs where required;
- performing prescribed exercises;
- understanding hip precautions.

For an international patient, these are **hospital discharge** criteria—not airline criteria.

---

# 11. Hip precautions and rehabilitation

AAOS notes that precautions can vary according to the operation and surgeon. Some patients may be asked to avoid certain positions or movements for a period.

The important content principle:
- never publish one rigid “no bending past X degrees for every hip patient” rule as universal;
- display the treating surgeon’s actual precautions in the patient journey.

Rehabilitation may include:
- walking progression;
- assistive devices;
- strength work;
- range-of-motion exercises;
- home-safety adaptations;
- later return to low-impact activity.

# 12. Risks to discuss

Potential arthroplasty risks include:
- infection;
- blood clot;
- dislocation;
- fracture;
- bleeding;
- nerve/vessel injury;
- leg-length perception/difference;
- implant loosening/wear;
- persistent pain;
- anaesthetic/medical complications;
- revision surgery.

Individual risk depends on health and surgical factors.

International-patient question:
**What happens if a complication begins after the patient returns home?**

# 14. How to shortlist a hip-replacement hospital

This is a case-matching exercise, not a ranking. Ask each hospital to confirm the exact branch, joint-replacement program, proposed procedure and consultant assigned after the records are reviewed. Verify the assigned clinician's current role on the hospital's own directory and ask who provides cover if that person is unavailable.

Program evidence can include a dedicated joint-replacement service, relevant imaging and rehabilitation, medical support for your other conditions, a clear implant process and a credible return-home follow-up pathway. A press release or directory listing alone does not establish suitability or predict an outcome.

# 16. Choosing the city

Do not choose Mumbai, Delhi/Gurugram, Bengaluru, Chennai or another city solely from a “medical tourism” list.

Evaluate:
- correct surgeon/team;
- direct or manageable route from origin;
- airport-to-hospital journey;
- accommodation near hospital;
- local rehab;
- companion;
- expected review schedule;
- cost;
- language/support;
- return-home route.

For limited mobility, small logistical differences can matter.

# 27. Primary versus revision hip replacement

A revision is not simply a repeat primary hip replacement.

The hospital may need to evaluate:
- why the previous implant is failing;
- implant position;
- loosening;
- dislocation;
- fracture;
- wear;
- bone loss;
- infection;
- previous surgical approach;
- implant manufacturer/components.

A revision case packet should ideally include:
- old operation note;
- implant labels/card;
- previous X-rays;
- current X-rays;
- CT if requested;
- inflammatory/infection testing if already done;
- aspiration/culture reports when relevant.

Canopus should tag revision cases separately so they are not routed into a primary-joint “package” workflow.

# 28. Why an international patient should ask about blood-clot prevention

Major lower-limb joint surgery is associated with blood-clot risk. The treating team may use medication, mechanical measures and early mobility based on the patient’s situation.

The Canopus page should **not** specify an individual anticoagulant or duration.

Instead, ask:
- what prevention plan the surgeon uses;
- whether it changes for long-haul travel;
- what symptoms should trigger urgent assessment;
- whether compression/mobility advice applies to the flight;
- whether the patient needs a review before flying.

This is one of the reasons a generic “fly home X days after surgery” claim is unsafe.
